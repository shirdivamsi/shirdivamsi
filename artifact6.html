Artifact 6

AI Incidents and Ethics

A Critical Analysis of Facial Recognition Bias and Accountability in Law Enforcement AI

Project Information

Course: Machine Learning Fundamentals

Artifact Type: AI Ethics Critical Thinking Assignment

Tools Used: Academic Research, APA Citation, Critical Analysis

Skills Demonstrated: Ethical Reasoning, Critical Thinking, Case Study Analysis, Accountability and Governance Awareness, Technical Communication

Project Overview

This artifact is a critical analysis of a real-world AI incident: the wrongful arrests of Black men in the United States between 2019 and 2021 following false matches from facial recognition systems, including the documented cases of Robert Williams and Michael Oliver in Detroit and Nijeer Parks in New Jersey. The assignment required selecting a documented AI incident, analyzing multiple underlying issues, working through my own reasoning process for each, and proposing a concrete solution grounded in governance and accountability rather than only technical fixes.

Objective

The objective of this assignment was to move beyond treating an AI failure as a purely technical problem, and instead evaluate the training data, deployment context, institutional standards, and legal accountability structures that allowed a flawed AI output to result in real harm to real people. This artifact serves as the closing piece of my AI and Machine Learning portfolio, connecting the technical foundations covered earlier in the program to the ethical responsibility that comes with applying them.

Case Study Summary

Between 2019 and 2021, several documented cases surfaced in the United States in which Black men, including Robert Williams and Michael Oliver in Detroit and Nijeer Parks in New Jersey, were falsely identified by facial recognition systems and subsequently arrested. In each case, police relied on a facial recognition match to identify a suspect, and the match proved incorrect, in some instances leading to extended detention. Research from NIST and the MIT Media Lab has independently shown that many commercially available facial recognition systems have significantly higher error rates for darker-skinned faces, particularly for women. Together, these cases point to a combination of algorithmic error and insufficient human oversight resulting in serious civil rights violations.

Critical Analysis

Issue 1: Algorithmic Bias and Differential Error Rates

My first instinct was to treat this as a pure accuracy problem, one that would resolve itself as the underlying technology improved. Looking more closely, the bias traced back to the training data itself, which was drawn largely from lighter-skinned populations, combined with inadequate demographic-specific testing before deployment. The error rates were known in advance and the systems were deployed regardless, which shifts this from a technical gap to a decision made with awareness of the risk.

Proposed solution: Require proof of comparable accuracy across race, gender, and age groups before any facial recognition system is approved for law enforcement use, and bar deployment in consequential contexts for any system that fails to meet that threshold for any group.

Issue 2: Inadequate Corroboration Standards in Law Enforcement Use

My initial framing was that the technology itself had failed. On further analysis, the more accurate issue was how the output was interpreted and acted on. A facial recognition match is a probability, not a confirmed identity, yet in these cases a partial match alone was treated as sufficient grounds for an arrest, a standard of evidence the technology was never designed to support.

Proposed solution: Prohibit facial recognition matches from serving as the sole or primary basis for an arrest, require independent corroborating evidence such as witness identification or physical evidence before a warrant is issued, and mandate that investigators using these systems be specifically trained on their real error rates and limitations.

Issue 3: Absence of Accountability When AI Systems Cause Harm

None of the documented wrongful arrest cases resulted in meaningful consequences for the vendor or the agency involved, despite real harm to the people affected, including detention, job loss, and reputational damage. Determining fault turned out to be genuinely unclear: vendors can point to use outside recommended guidelines, and agencies can point to reliance on vendor expertise, leaving a gap where no one bears responsibility and there is little incentive to improve.

Proposed solution: Establish clear civil liability frameworks that hold AI vendors and the agencies deploying their systems jointly accountable for harm caused by facial recognition misidentification, with a defined legal remedy available to affected individuals and liability insurance or bonding required in proportion to deployment scale.

Reflection on Critical Thinking

I wrote this artifact for two audiences: my instructor, evaluating whether my analysis went beyond surface-level outrage into structural reasoning, and future portfolio readers, including employers, who would want to see that I can reason about AI responsibly and not only technically. That is why each issue above is framed as a concrete problem paired with a specific governance-based solution, rather than a general statement that "bias is bad" or "AI needs oversight."

My thinking changed substantially over the course of this analysis. I started out treating facial recognition misidentification as a technical accuracy problem to be solved by a better model. Working through it issue by issue pushed me toward a much broader view that includes training data diversity, deployment context, institutional standards for corroboration, and legal accountability. The assumption I had to give up was that law enforcement would naturally verify an AI output independently before acting on it. The documented cases show that assumption does not hold, and that gap between how the technology is meant to be used and how it is actually used is where the real harm occurred.

This reasoning will directly shape how I approach AI work going forward. I will treat deployment context, not just model performance, as part of the technical design itself, since a model that tests well can still cause serious harm if deployed into an environment without adequate protocols. I plan to advocate for end-to-end ethical review, covering use context and governance alongside accuracy, rather than treating model performance as the only measure of whether a system is ready to deploy.

Future Applications

This closing artifact ties together the technical foundation built throughout this portfolio, from AI history, to model training methods, to data-driven decision scenarios, with the ethical responsibility that comes with applying AI in real settings. As I continue toward hands-on AI and Machine Learning work, I intend to treat context, deployment risk, and accountability as part of the design process itself, not an afterthought evaluated only once a system underperforms.
